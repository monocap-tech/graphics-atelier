# G06-F03 — Material manufacture of the monochrome manga image

Date: 2026-11-18. **Status: source-led foundations, R1 for documented practice and historical report. No R3 original execution.** Continues [F01](G06-FUNDAMENTALS-01.md) and [F02](G06-FUNDAMENTALS-02.md). The user directed an orderly examination of manga practices before animation, illustration rigging and games. No prototype, new editor/tool standard, art commission, analog physical experiment, print-proof, copyright acquisition or audience study conducted. The actual URLs/text were inspected 2026-11-18; film and original manuscript visuals have not been systematically measured. Do not infer game-production ancestry from similarities.

## Question

What operations on paper, ink, patterned film and reproduction produce the visible line, solid-black masses, hatching, apparent gray, highlighted forms and intentional atmospheric graphics in monochrome manga? What happens when those operations are transferred to modern layers and print/display output? **Distinguish four things:** (1) physical authoring operation, (2) produced graphical form, (3) position/function within a page, and (4) artist's intended/actual spectator response. None is interchangeable.

## Construction sequence — operation, result, constraint

**1. Substrate / registration.** Comic manuscript sheet carries trim/bleed/gutter, frame and sometimes printer marks. Printed work differs from original artwork and from an on-screen preview. Shueisha's submission and independent manual distinguish physical no-touch/crop precautions; F02 already established page enclosure. Exact paper thickness and ink chemistry deliberately **not** guessed from generic tutorials.

**2. Line / pen touch.** G-pen, maru pen, brush and technical/felt pens produce different lines with varying line-width, stroke starts/ends and density. A contour is simultaneously anatomical delineation, tonal weight and rhythmic graphic presence. Change of nib does not automatically imply a different meaning or national stylistic convention.

**3. Solid-black treatment.** ベタ fills a region; ツヤベタ composes blacks and selectively reserved/tapered highlights to produce gloss, materially/identically distinctive hair or hard light. White correction can recover or add brightness, but is not the same operation as initially reserving paper white. One real creator assigns a distinguishing identity role to glossy black (Nakatani); reader perception not measured.

**4. Manual line textures / カケアミ.** Repeated hand-controlled line groups can create density and contour/material rhythm. A manufacturer-supplied preset kakeami film may resemble the distribution but not necessarily preserve an artist's stroke variation or revision method. Hamano's 7 mm / 5–6-stroke grid is a local training exercise, not empirical page-grid evidence for all Japan. Distinguish direct handmade marks from raster print cell phenomena.

**5. Tone as manufactured pattern.** Analog 透明粘着フィルム has a preprinted regular dot/line or picture motif: lay over manuscript, cut, attach, trim, rub; scraping removes printed pigment to reshape distribution; layering combines pattern fields (can cause deliberate or accidental moiré). Tone can be material, volume, lighting, atmosphere, figurative motif or procedural economy. Not every printed pattern is a semantic code. Meiji museum provides **the material-specific dated lineage** and Kochi exhibition gives multiple differentiated examples.

**6. Correction and negative drawing.** Opaque white repairs mistaken black, adds glints, contours, special highlights; it can participate in the composition rather than only undo mistakes. Black-first vs white-preserving strategy changes planning burdens. Ink may be blocked by film; Shueisha recommends drawing under the tone then removing tone where necessary. Restoration process has practical reversibility and surface-layer limits.

**7. Printed or displayed artifact.** A dot pattern has native repetition rate and angle/relative phase; export scaling or halftoning grayscale into a mono bitmap alters its effective structure. CELSYS explains moiré from overlapping dot grids and resampling. Display backlight/print reflectance is an artist's noted viewing difference; no device-level measurement. A digital alpha gray and a black/white dot screen may have similar *average* luminance at one size but different texture, edges and behavior under scaling. This is an important possible later image→sprite/display bridge, not yet tested.

## Historical observation worth preserving

The exhibition's **specific** chronology is more probative than broad claims of manga/anime ancestry:

- A graphic-design/technical sheet material became available to manga artists; a reported individual adoption around the mid-1950s spread among peers.
- The location of printed pattern on the film **changed the affordances** around 1970: front-surface printing supports scraping off pigments and making local graduations/whites.
- Manufacturers, independent creators and amateur/dōjin markets participated in expanding the repertory and material availability. This is a documented process **within manga**, not evidence that later game's particle effects were derived from it.

Do not claim tone was invented in Japan, that Nagata was the absolute first artist worldwide, that every magazine adopted it in the same years, or that a purchased tone was used directly by anime or arcade effects designers without other evidence.

## Two comparators in the corpus (observations, not new images)

**F03-C1: Nakatani's character hair vs other black hair.** Same general physical treatment class (dark hair), selectively differentiated ツヤベタ; creator testifies it intentionally marks a specific character. The decisive authoring object is the *pattern of gloss within a black fill*, not a preset called 'sparkle'. Actual paired manga panels and blind recognition are not audited; identity impression is artist's goal.

**F03-C2: Sato's streetlamp scene under analog/digital construction.** Analog approach planned untouched white around a lamp before completing dark environment; digital build paints/stages light after full background and changes opacity. Both target an illuminated night scene but planning and later revision affordances differ. Artist warns that monochrome print conversion may compromise contrast. Published article text and its described image stages inspected; no independently captured prints or quantitatively matched outputs. Artist's demonstration is a technique/strategy comparison, not a controlled study of viewer response.
